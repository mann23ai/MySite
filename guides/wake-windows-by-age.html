Wake windows by age

How long a baby comfortably stays awake between sleeps — and why the number you find online is a starting point, not a target.

Updated 27 August 20266 min readSourced & cited

A wake window is the stretch of time a baby is awake between one sleep and the next. It has become the standard way parents talk about infant sleep, and it is genuinely useful — but it is worth being clear about what kind of thing it is.

Wake windows are not part of any formal clinical sleep guideline. What the sleep and pediatric bodies publish is total sleep in 24 hours, which is a different measurement entirely. Typical wake-window ranges are published by medical centres as practical guidance rather than as a standard — the ones below come from Cleveland Clinic, where pediatrician Dr. Kristin Barrett describes them as a ballpark, not a rule.

Typical wake windows, birth to twelve months

Awake time between sleeps

Birth to 1 month
30 min – 1 hr
1 to 3 months
1 – 2 hrs
3 to 4 months
1¼ – 2½ hrs
5 to 7 months
2 – 4 hrs
7 to 10 months
2½ – 4½ hrs
10 to 12 months
3 – 6 hrs

Notice how wide those ranges get. At ten months the range spans three hours, which is another way of saying that babies of the same age genuinely differ. As Dr. Barrett puts it: “Babies are individuals. So, one baby’s wake window may look a little different from another baby’s.”

What the guidelines actually say

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine publishes recommended sleep durations, and the American Academy of Pediatrics supports them. For the first year:

  • Infants 4 to 12 months: 12 to 16 hours per 24 hours, including naps.
  • Children 1 to 2 years: 11 to 14 hours per 24 hours, including naps.

Two things follow from that. First, naps count — a baby who sleeps ten hours overnight and three in the day is inside the range. Second, the recommendation is about the daily total, not about how that total is divided up. There is no official position on how long any individual stretch of wakefulness should be.

Why your baby’s own pattern beats a chart

A published range is an average across many babies. Your baby is one baby. If the chart says two hours and yours reliably melts down at ninety minutes, the chart is not describing your child — and the useful number was in your own logs the whole time.

This is why watching your baby matters more than watching the clock. Rubbing eyes, staring past you, losing interest in things that were interesting a minute ago: those cues tell you where this baby’s limit is today. Dr. Barrett’s advice is to “watch your baby’s cues to start getting a feel for when their wake window is hitting its limit.”

When wake windows shift

They lengthen as babies grow, but rarely smoothly. Expect the pattern to move around illness, teeth, travel, and developmental leaps, and expect it to move backwards sometimes. A week of shorter wake windows during a cold is not a regression; it is a baby with a cold.

This is general information, not medical advice. Every baby is different, and the ranges here describe what is typical — not what is required. Your pediatrician knows your child; a web page does not. If something worries you, call them. No concern is too small.
