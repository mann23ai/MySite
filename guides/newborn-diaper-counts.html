How many diapers is normal

In the first week, diapers are the clearest signal that feeding is going well. Here are the numbers the American Academy of Pediatrics gives, day by day.

Updated 27 August 20265 min readSourced & cited

Newborns cannot tell you they are getting enough milk, and in the first week you cannot weigh them at home. What you can do is count diapers. Output is the most practical proxy for intake there is, which is why it is the first thing a pediatrician asks about.

The first week, day by day

What the AAP describes as expected

Days 1–2
1–2 stools daily, blackish and tarry
Days 3–4
At least 2 stools, turning greenish–yellow
Days 5–7 onward
3–4+ yellow, loose stools daily
By 5–7 days old
6 or more wet diapers daily

The colour progression is doing real work here. Those first blackish, tarry stools are meconium, which was in the gut before birth. Moving from black to green to yellow over the first several days is the visible sign that milk is going in and moving through.

By five to seven days, the AAP describes urine that is “nearly colorless or pale yellow.” Dark or strongly coloured urine in a newborn is worth a phone call.

Weight loss in the first days is expected

Newborns lose weight before they gain it. The AAP’s figure is that a baby should “lose no more than 8%–10% of their birth weight in the first few days after birth, before starting to gain weight again.”

Losing some weight is normal. It is the size of the loss, and whether the gain starts, that your pediatrician is watching — which is one reason the first well-child visit is scheduled so early.

After the first weeks, the numbers loosen

Stool frequency in particular varies a great deal between babies once feeding is established, and your pediatrician is the right person to tell you what is normal for yours. Wet diapers stay the steadier day-to-day signal.

This is why counting matters more than any single day’s total. One quiet day means little. A pattern — three days of noticeably fewer wet diapers — is information worth having in front of you when you call.

When to call

Call your pediatrician if wet diapers drop below the expected count, if urine is dark, if your baby seems unusually sleepy or hard to rouse for feeds, or if you are simply not sure. Newborn feeding problems are common, fixable, and much easier to fix early.

This is general information, not medical advice. Every baby is different, and the ranges here describe what is typical — not what is required. Your pediatrician knows your child; a web page does not. If something worries you, call them. No concern is too small.
